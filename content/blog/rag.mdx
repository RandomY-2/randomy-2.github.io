## Introduction

Large pre-trained language models store factual knowledge in their parameters and achieve strong results when fine-tuned on downstream NLP tasks. Their ability to access and precisely manipulate that knowledge is still limited. On knowledge-intensive tasks — tasks that humans could not reasonably be expected to perform without an external knowledge source — parametric models lag behind task-specific architectures. Providing provenance for their decisions and updating their world knowledge remain open problems. Models of this kind cannot easily expand or revise their memory, cannot straightforwardly explain a prediction, and may produce hallucinations.

Hybrid models that combine parametric memory with non-parametric (retrieval-based) memory address some of these issues: knowledge can be directly revised and expanded, and accessed knowledge can be inspected. REALM and ORQA combine masked language models with a differentiable retriever and show promising results, but only on open-domain extractive question answering. RAG brings the same hybrid memory to seq2seq generation.

The parametric memory is a pre-trained seq2seq transformer (BART). The non-parametric memory is a dense vector index of Wikipedia, accessed with a pre-trained neural retriever (DPR). The two are combined in a probabilistic model trained end-to-end. The retriever provides latent documents conditioned on the input; the generator conditions on those documents together with the input to produce the output. The latent documents are marginalized with a top-$K$ approximation, either once per output sequence (the same document is responsible for all tokens) or once per token (different documents can be responsible for different tokens). Like T5 or BART, RAG can be fine-tuned on any seq2seq task, with the generator and retriever learned jointly.

This is different from architectures trained from scratch for a specific task — memory networks, stack-augmented networks, memory layers. Both the parametric and non-parametric components are pre-trained and pre-loaded with extensive knowledge. Because the access mechanism is itself pre-trained, the ability to access knowledge is present without additional training.

Results:

* State of the art on open Natural Questions, WebQuestions, and CuratedTrec, outperforming both parametric seq2seq models and task-specific retrieve-and-extract architectures. On TriviaQA, RAG strongly outperforms recent approaches that use specialized pre-training objectives. Unconstrained generation outperforms previous extractive approaches even though the tasks are extractive.
* On knowledge-intensive generation (MS-MARCO and Jeopardy question generation), RAG produces responses that are more factual, specific, and diverse than a BART baseline.
* On FEVER fact verification, results are within 4.3% of state-of-the-art pipeline models that use strong retrieval supervision.
* The non-parametric memory can be replaced to update the model's knowledge as the world changes, without retraining.

## Methods

RAG uses the input sequence $x$ to retrieve text documents $z$ and uses them as additional context when generating the target sequence $y$. There are two components: (i) a retriever $p_\eta(z \mid x)$ that returns a top-$K$ truncated distribution over text passages given a query $x$, and (ii) a generator $p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1})$ that produces the current token from the previous tokens, the original input, and a retrieved passage.

The retrieved document is treated as a latent variable so that the retriever and generator can be trained end-to-end. Two models marginalize over the latent documents in different ways.

### Models

**RAG-Sequence** uses the same retrieved document to generate the complete sequence. The retrieved document is a single latent variable, marginalized with a top-$K$ approximation:

$$
p_{\text{RAG-Sequence}}(y \mid x) \approx \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k(p(\cdot \mid x))} p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)
$$

$$
= \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k(p(\cdot \mid x))} p_\eta(z \mid x) \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1})
$$

**RAG-Token** can draw a different latent document for each target token. The generator can therefore choose content from several documents when producing an answer:

$$
p_{\text{RAG-Token}}(y \mid x) \approx \prod_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k(p(\cdot \mid x))} p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1})
$$

For sequence classification, the target class is a sequence of length one, and the two models are equivalent.

### Retriever: DPR

The retrieval component is Dense Passage Retriever. DPR is a bi-encoder:

$$
p_\eta(z \mid x) \propto \exp\big(\mathbf{d}(z)^\top \mathbf{q}(x)\big), \qquad \mathbf{d}(z) = \text{BERT}_d(z), \quad \mathbf{q}(x) = \text{BERT}_q(x)
$$

where $\mathbf{d}(z)$ is a dense representation of a document from a $\text{BERT}_{\text{BASE}}$ document encoder and $\mathbf{q}(x)$ is a query representation from a query encoder, also $\text{BERT}_{\text{BASE}}$. Computing the top-$k$ documents is a Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) problem, solvable approximately in sub-linear time. A pre-trained DPR bi-encoder initializes the retriever and builds the document index — the non-parametric memory. That retriever was trained to retrieve documents that contain answers to TriviaQA and Natural Questions.

### Generator: BART

The generator can be any encoder-decoder. RAG uses BART-large, a 400M-parameter pre-trained seq2seq transformer. The input $x$ and the retrieved content $z$ are concatenated when generating. BART was pre-trained with a denoising objective and a variety of noising functions; it has state-of-the-art results on a diverse set of generation tasks and outperforms comparably sized T5 models. The BART parameters $\theta$ are the parametric memory.

### Training

The retriever and generator are jointly trained with no direct supervision on which document should be retrieved. Given a fine-tuning corpus of pairs $(x_j, y_j)$, the objective is the negative marginal log-likelihood $\sum_j -\log p(y_j \mid x_j)$, minimized with Adam.

Updating the document encoder $\text{BERT}_d$ during training is costly: the document index would need to be rebuilt periodically, as REALM does during pre-training. That step is not necessary for strong performance. The document encoder and index stay fixed; only the query encoder $\text{BERT}_q$ and the BART generator are fine-tuned.

### Decoding

At test time the two models need different approximations to $\arg\max_y p(y \mid x)$.

**RAG-Token** is a standard autoregressive seq2seq generator with transition probability

$$
p'_\theta(y_i \mid x, y_{1:i-1}) = \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k(p(\cdot \mid x))} p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1})
$$

This plugs into a standard beam decoder.

**RAG-Sequence** does not factor into a conventional per-token likelihood, so a single beam search is not enough. Beam search is run for each document $z$, scoring hypotheses with $p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1})$. This yields a candidate set $Y$; some hypotheses may not have appeared in every document's beam. To estimate $p(y \mid x)$, an additional forward pass is run for each document $z$ for which $y$ is missing from the beam, the generator probability is multiplied by $p_\eta(z \mid x)$, and the probabilities are summed. This is **Thorough Decoding**.

For longer outputs, $|Y|$ can become large. A cheaper approximation sets $p_\theta(y \mid x, z_i) \approx 0$ when $y$ was not generated during beam search from $(x, z_i)$, avoiding extra forward passes once $Y$ is built. This is **Fast Decoding**.

## Experiments

All experiments use a single Wikipedia dump as the non-parametric knowledge source — the December 2018 dump, following Lee et al. and Karpukhin et al. Each article is split into disjoint 100-word chunks, producing 21M documents. The document encoder embeds each chunk; a FAISS MIPS index with Hierarchical Navigable Small World search provides fast retrieval. Training retrieves the top $k$ documents per query, with $k \in \{5, 10\}$; test-time $k$ is selected on dev data.

### Open-domain Question Answering

Open-domain QA is a common testbed for knowledge-intensive tasks. Questions and answers are treated as input-output pairs $(x, y)$, and RAG is trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of answers.

Two families of baselines: the extractive QA paradigm, where answers are spans from retrieved documents and knowledge is primarily non-parametric; and closed-book QA, which generates answers from parametric knowledge alone, with no retrieval.

Four datasets: Natural Questions (NQ), TriviaQA (TQA), WebQuestions (WQ), and CuratedTrec (CT). WQ and CT are small, so those models are initialized from the NQ RAG model, following DPR. Splits match prior work. The metric is Exact Match. For TriviaQA, results are also reported on the TQA Wiki test set to compare with T5.

### Abstractive Question Answering

RAG can go beyond extractive QA and answer with free-form text. The test is MSMARCO NLG v2.1: questions, ten gold passages from a search engine, and a full-sentence answer annotated from those passages. The supplied passages are not used — only questions and answers — so MSMARCO is treated as open-domain abstractive QA.

Some questions cannot match the reference without the gold passages (e.g. "What is the weather in Volcano, CA?"). Some cannot be answered from Wikipedia alone. In those cases RAG can fall back on parametric knowledge.

### Jeopardy Question Generation

To evaluate generation outside QA, the task is generating Jeopardy questions. Jeopardy guesses an entity from a fact about that entity: "The World Cup" is the answer to "In 1986 Mexico scored as the first country to host this international sports competition twice." The questions are precise factual statements, so generating them from answer entities is a demanding knowledge-intensive generation task.

Splits come from SearchQA: 100K train, 14K dev, 27K test. A BART model is trained for comparison. Automatic evaluation uses SQuAD-tuned Q-BLEU-1, a BLEU variant with higher weight for matching entities and higher correlation with human judgment for question generation. Two human evaluations assess factuality (whether a statement can be corroborated by trusted external sources) and specificity (high mutual dependence between input and output), using pairwise comparative evaluation. Annotators see an answer and two generated questions — one from BART, one from RAG — and pick: A better, B better, both good, or neither good.

### Fact Verification

FEVER classifies whether a natural language claim is supported or refuted by Wikipedia, or whether there is not enough information. The task requires retrieving evidence and reasoning over it. It is also a test of RAG as a classifier rather than a generator.

Class labels (supports, refutes, not enough info) are mapped to single output tokens and trained as claim–class pairs. Unlike most FEVER systems, there is no supervision on retrieved evidence. In many real-world applications such supervision is unavailable.

Two variants: standard 3-way classification, and the 2-way (supports/refutes) task of Thorne and Vlachos. The metric is label accuracy.

## Results

### Open-domain Question Answering

On all four open-domain QA tasks, RAG sets a new state of the art (on the T5-comparable split for TriviaQA). It combines the generation flexibility of closed-book models with the performance of open-book retrieval.

| | Model | NQ | TQA | WQ | CT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Closed book | T5-11B | 34.5 | — / 50.1 | 37.4 | — |
| | T5-11B + SSM | 36.6 | — / 60.5 | 44.7 | — |
| Open book | REALM | 40.4 | — / — | 40.7 | 46.8 |
| | DPR | 41.5 | 57.9 / — | 41.1 | 50.6 |
| | RAG-Token | 44.1 | 55.2 / 66.1 | 45.5 | 50.0 |
| | RAG-Sequence | **44.5** | 56.8 / **68.0** | 45.2 | **52.2** |

Unlike REALM and T5+SSM, RAG does not need expensive salient-span-masking pre-training. The retriever is initialized from DPR, which uses retrieval supervision on NQ and TriviaQA. RAG still compares favorably to the full DPR QA system, which uses a BERT cross-encoder to re-rank documents plus an extractive reader. Neither a re-ranker nor an extractive reader is necessary for state-of-the-art performance.

Generating answers has advantages even when extraction is possible. Documents that contain clues but not the answer verbatim can still contribute to a correct generation — they cannot contribute under standard extractive methods — which makes marginalization over documents more effective. RAG can also generate a correct answer when that answer is in none of the retrieved documents, reaching 11.8% accuracy in such cases on NQ, where an extractive model would score 0%.

### Abstractive Question Answering

RAG-Sequence outperforms BART on open MS-MARCO NLG by 2.6 BLEU and 2.6 ROUGE-L. That approaches state-of-the-art models which (i) access gold passages containing the information needed for the reference, (ii) face questions that are unanswerable without those passages, and (iii) include questions that Wikipedia alone cannot answer. Qualitatively, RAG hallucinates less and generates factually correct text more often than BART. Generations are also more diverse (§ Additional Results).

### Jeopardy Question Generation

RAG-Token outperforms RAG-Sequence on Jeopardy question generation; both beat BART on Q-BLEU-1.

| Model | Jeopardy B-1 | Jeopardy QB-1 | MSMARCO R-L | MSMARCO B-1 | FEVER-3 | FEVER-2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SotA | — | — | 49.8* | 49.9* | 76.8 | 92.2* |
| BART | 15.1 | 19.7 | 38.2 | 41.6 | 64.0 | 81.1 |
| RAG-Token | **17.3** | **22.2** | 40.1 | 41.5 | **72.5** | **89.5** |
| RAG-Sequence | 14.7 | 21.4 | **40.8** | **44.2** | | |

\*Uses gold context or evidence.

Human evaluation over 452 pairs of BART and RAG-Token generations:

| | Factuality | Specificity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BART better | 7.1% | 16.8% |
| RAG better | 42.7% | 37.4% |
| Both good | 11.7% | 11.8% |
| Both poor | 17.7% | 6.9% |
| No majority | 20.8% | 20.1% |

BART is more factual than RAG in only 7.1% of cases; RAG is more factual in 42.7%, and both are factual in a further 17%. Specificity favors RAG by a similar margin.

Jeopardy questions often contain two separate pieces of information. RAG-Token may perform best because it can combine content from several documents. Figure 2 shows an example for the input "Hemingway." When generating "Sun", the posterior is high for the document that mentions *The Sun Also Rises*. When generating "A Farewell to Arms", the other document dominates. After the first token of each title, the posterior flattens: the generator can complete the titles from parametric memory. Feeding BART the partial decoding "The Sun" yields "The Sun Also Rises is a novel by this author of The Sun Also Rises" — the title is stored in BART's parameters. The same holds for *A Farewell to Arms*. Parametric and non-parametric memory work together: retrieval guides generation and draws out specific knowledge stored in the parameters.

Typical generations: for "Washington", BART produces the false "This state has the largest number of counties in the U.S."; RAG-Token produces "It's the only U.S. state named for a U.S. president." For "define middle ear", BART hallucinates "the part of the ear between the middle ear and the nose"; RAG-Sequence produces "The middle ear includes the tympanic cavity and the three ossicles."

### Fact Verification

On 3-way FEVER, RAG is within 4.3% of state-of-the-art models — complex pipelines with domain-specific architectures, substantial engineering, and intermediate retrieval supervision, which RAG does not use. On 2-way classification, RAG is within 2.7% of a RoBERTa model that classifies the claim given the gold evidence sentence, despite being supplied with only the claim and retrieving its own evidence.

Retrieved documents overlap gold FEVER evidence: the top document is from a gold article in 71% of cases, and a gold article is in the top 10 in 90% of cases.

### Additional Results

**Generation diversity.** Distinct-trigram ratio (distinct / total) without any diversity-promoting decoding:

| | MSMARCO | Jeopardy QGen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gold | 89.6% | 90.0% |
| BART | 70.7% | 32.4% |
| RAG-Token | 77.8% | 46.8% |
| RAG-Sequence | **83.5%** | **53.8%** |

RAG-Sequence is more diverse than RAG-Token; both are substantially more diverse than BART.

**Retrieval ablations.** Freezing the retriever during training hurts every task — learned retrieval improves results. Replacing the dense retriever with a fixed BM25 system, using BM25 scores as logits for $p(z \mid x)$, is best on FEVER (claims are heavily entity-centric and well suited to word overlap) but worse on every other task, especially open-domain QA, where differentiable retrieval is crucial.

**Index hot-swapping.** Non-parametric memory can be updated at test time. Parametric-only models need further training as the world changes. An index built on the DrQA Wikipedia dump from December 2016 is compared with the December 2018 index from the main results. A list of 82 world leaders who changed between those dates is queried with templates of the form "Who is the President of Peru?" using the NQ RAG model. Accuracy is 70% with the 2016 index on 2016 leaders and 68% with the 2018 index on 2018 leaders. Mismatched indices collapse: 12% (2018 index, 2016 leaders) and 4% (2016 index, 2018 leaders). World knowledge updates by replacing the non-parametric memory.

**Effect of retrieving more documents.** Models train with 5 or 10 latent documents; the two settings are not significantly different. Test-time $k$ can still be adjusted. Retrieving more documents at test time monotonically improves open-domain QA for RAG-Sequence; RAG-Token peaks at 10 documents. On MS-MARCO, more documents raise ROUGE-L for RAG-Token at the expense of BLEU-1; the effect is weaker for RAG-Sequence.

## Related Work

### Single-Task Retrieval

Retrieval improves performance across many NLP tasks when each task is considered in isolation: open-domain QA, fact checking, fact completion, long-form QA, Wikipedia article generation, dialogue, translation, and language modeling. RAG unifies those successes: a single retrieval-based architecture achieves strong performance across several tasks.

### General-Purpose Architectures for NLP

General-purpose architectures have succeeded without retrieval. A single pre-trained language model, after fine-tuning, is strong on GLUE classification. GPT-2 showed that one left-to-right LM can cover both discriminative and generative tasks. BART and T5 then used a pre-trained encoder-decoder with bidirectional attention for stronger performance on both. RAG expands the space of tasks a single architecture can cover by learning a retrieval module that augments a pre-trained generative model.

### Learned Retrieval

Learning to retrieve documents is a long-standing IR problem, more recently with pre-trained neural models similar to DPR. Some work optimizes retrieval for a specific downstream task using search, reinforcement learning, or a latent-variable approach as here. Those successes use different architectures and optimization for a single task. RAG shows that one retrieval-based architecture can be fine-tuned for strong performance on a variety of tasks.

### Memory-based Architectures

The document index is a large external memory for the network to attend to, analogous to memory networks. Concurrent work learns to retrieve a trained embedding for each entity rather than raw text. Other work improves factual dialogue by attending over fact embeddings. A key property of this memory is that it is raw text, not distributed representations: it is human-readable (a form of interpretability) and human-writable (the memory can be updated by editing the index). Conditioning generators on retrieved text has also been used in knowledge-intensive dialogue, though there the text came from TF-IDF rather than end-to-end learned retrieval.

### Retrieve-and-Edit Approaches

Retrieve-and-edit methods retrieve a similar training input-output pair and edit it to produce the final output. They have been successful in machine translation and semantic parsing. RAG differs in emphasizing aggregation over several retrieved pieces of evidence rather than lightly editing one retrieved item, in learning latent retrieval, and in retrieving evidence documents rather than related training pairs. RAG-style techniques may still work well in those settings.

## Discussion

Hybrid generation models with access to parametric and non-parametric memory obtain state-of-the-art results on open-domain QA. People prefer RAG's generations over purely parametric BART, finding RAG more factual and specific. The learned retrieval component is effective, and the retrieval index can be hot-swapped to update the model without retraining.

A natural next step is to investigate whether the two components can be jointly pre-trained from scratch, either with a BART-style denoising objective or another objective. The interaction between parametric and non-parametric memories — and how to combine them most effectively — is an open research direction, with promise across a wide variety of NLP tasks.

## Broader Impact

Grounding in a real knowledge source (here Wikipedia) reduces hallucination, produces more factual generations, and offers more control and interpretability. The same recipe could be employed with a medical index for open-domain questions on that topic, or to help people be more effective at their jobs.

Wikipedia, or any external knowledge source, will probably never be entirely factual or free of bias. Because RAG can be used as a language model, concerns similar to those for GPT-2 apply — generating abuse, faked or misleading content, impersonation, spam and phishing — though arguably to a lesser extent. Advanced language models may also automate jobs. Mitigations include using AI systems to fight misleading content and automated spam.
